Exit & capital

Due diligence readiness: what a buyer or investor actually checks first

Most SMEs find out what due diligence requires at the worst possible time — after a buyer or investor has already asked for it.

Due diligence isn't really a test of whether the business is good. Most of the businesses that go through it are perfectly good businesses. It's a test of whether the business can prove it — quickly, consistently, and without the owner having to reconstruct half the answer from memory the week a buyer or investor actually asks. That distinction is the whole game. A business can be genuinely sound and still lose weeks of momentum, or several points of price, because the evidence for it wasn't sitting in order when someone came looking.

The financial side of due diligence usually starts in the same place regardless of whether the process is a sale, an acquisition, or a capital raise: three to five years of profit and loss statements, balance sheets and management accounts, reconciled against the lodged tax returns and the bank statements rather than presented on their own. Aged receivables and payables broken out, not buried in a single line. Any related-party transactions — a related entity, a family loan, an owner's personal expenses running through the business — disclosed and explained rather than discovered. None of this is unusual to ask for. What varies enormously is how long it takes a given business to produce it, and that gap is almost entirely a function of whether the numbers were kept audit-ready all along or assembled under pressure once the question was asked.

What tends to cost time, or price, when it's missing

A handful of gaps show up again and again, and they're rarely fatal on their own — but each one either slows the process down or gives the other side a reason to chip at the number. Customer concentration that was never addressed, where one or two accounts are a large enough share of revenue to worry a buyer about what happens the day they don't renew. Contracts that don't survive a change of control, or were never checked for a clause that requires the other party's consent before ownership can transfer. Unpaid or understated superannuation, payroll tax or leave liabilities — common exposures that surface in exactly this kind of review and are expensive to discover late rather than early. And a working capital position nobody can explain cleanly: debtor days drifting out, stock sitting longer than it should, cash tied up in ways the management accounts don't make obvious at a glance.

Why this is cheaper to fix before the process starts than during it

Every one of these is fixable. The only variable is when it gets fixed. Addressed months ahead of a raise or a sale, a customer concentration problem is a strategic conversation about diversifying the base — the same conversation that sits behind what actually raises enterprise value rather than just profit. Found by a buyer's advisers three weeks into a deal, it's a negotiating point used against the price, and there's no time left to do anything about it except accept the discount. The same is true of the broader reporting discipline underneath all of it: a business already producing board- and bank-grade numbers as a matter of routine walks into due diligence with most of the pack already built. A business assembling it for the first time under deal pressure is doing weeks of unfamiliar work at the exact moment it has the least room to get it wrong.

The honest test of due diligence readiness isn't whether the business could eventually produce everything a buyer or investor would ask for. Given enough time, most businesses could. It's whether it could produce it this month, in good order, without the request itself becoming the reason the deal slows down. Getting there isn't a sprint before a transaction — it's the same financial discipline that should have been running the business anyway, just with someone checking, early enough, that it would actually hold up under someone else's scrutiny.
