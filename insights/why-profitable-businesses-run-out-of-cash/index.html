Cash & reporting

Why profitable businesses run out of cash

Profit and cash are different things, and growth usually widens the gap between them rather than closing it.

It's one of the more disorienting things an owner can hear from their own bank balance: the P&L says this has been a good year, and the account says otherwise. This is the complaint every accountant hears sooner or later — profitable but cash poor, with no obvious reason why a stronger year feels tighter than a weaker one did. The business isn't broken and the numbers aren't wrong. Profit and cash are simply two different measurements, and the gap between them usually widens exactly when things are going well, not when they're going badly.

Profit is an accounting figure. It's recognised when a sale is made or an invoice is raised, not when the money actually lands in the account. Cash is what's actually sitting there to pay wages, suppliers and the tax office. A business can be genuinely, provably profitable on every measure an accountant would sign off on, and still be weeks away from a cash shortage — because none of that profit has turned into cash yet.

Where the gap actually comes from

Growth is usually the biggest driver, not the exception most owners expect. A business that wins more work has to fund the wages, materials and subcontractors for that work before the invoice is raised, then wait again — often 30, 60 or 90 days — for the invoice to be paid. The faster the business grows, the more of its own cash gets tied up funding work that hasn't been paid for yet, and the profit sitting on the P&L sits in debtors rather than in the bank. Debtor days creeping out while creditor terms stay tight makes it worse: paying suppliers faster than customers pay the business is a quiet, compounding drain that a monthly profit figure never shows. Stock and work-in-progress do the same thing from a different angle — cash spent weeks ago, revenue not recognised until it sells or completes.

Why the P&L doesn't catch it in time

A P&L is a rear-view mirror, built to answer "did we make money" — it was never designed to answer "will we have enough cash in six weeks." By the time a monthly profit report shows a problem, the cash position has usually been tight for a month or more already. The businesses that get caught out aren't usually the unprofitable ones; they're the ones that assumed profitable meant safe, and found out otherwise the week a large supplier payment and a slow-paying client landed in the same fortnight.

The fix isn't more profit — it's visibility into the timing. A rolling cash flow forecast, reviewed monthly rather than built from scratch under pressure, turns an invisible timing gap into something that can be planned around: which month is tight, what's driving it, and what lever actually closes it — collecting faster, renegotiating supplier terms, or arranging facility headroom before it's needed rather than after. The same handful of forward-looking KPIs that catch a margin problem early — debtor days, creditor days, cash runway — are usually the first place this particular problem shows up, well before it reaches the bank balance. A business growing fast enough to need outside funding to cover the gap is worth asking which kind of funding actually fits, before the cash runs short enough to make that decision under pressure instead of on its own terms.

Being profitable and being solvent are both necessary, and neither one implies the other. The businesses that get caught out are usually the ones that only ever checked the first.
