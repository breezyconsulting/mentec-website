Cash & reporting

The financial KPIs an SME should actually be tracking

Revenue and profit are lagging indicators. A handful of forward-looking financial KPIs catch trouble before the P&L does.

Most SME reporting revolves around two numbers: revenue and profit. Both matter, but both are lagging indicators — they describe what already happened, not what's about to. By the time a monthly P&L shows a problem, the business has usually been living inside that problem for a month or two already. A small set of forward-looking financial KPIs, watched consistently rather than glanced at once, closes that gap.

The instinct when this comes up is to build a bigger dashboard. That's usually the wrong move. Most owners who go looking for "the right KPIs" end up with a spreadsheet of fifteen or twenty numbers that get reviewed once, look impressive, and then quietly stop being updated. The KPIs that actually change decisions are a small set that tend to move before the P&L does — and the habit of reading them as a trend, not a single month's snapshot.

The numbers that tend to move first

Cash runway — how many weeks or months the business can keep operating at its current burn rate if nothing changes. It's a different question to "are we profitable," and it's the one that actually determines how much time there is to react. Gross margin trend — not the absolute figure, but the direction over the last several months. Margin compressing quietly while revenue grows is one of the more common ways a business ends up busier and worse off at the same time. Debtor days and creditor days — how long it actually takes to collect from customers versus how long the business has to pay its own suppliers. A widening gap between the two is a working capital problem showing up long before it shows up as a cash shortage. Recurring revenue as a share of total revenue — a number that tends to predict how stable next quarter looks, independent of how good this quarter was.

Why fewer numbers, watched properly, beats more numbers watched once

A dashboard only works if someone actually looks at it and reacts to what it shows. Four or five KPIs, reviewed monthly against their own trend line, will catch a developing cash or margin problem far earlier than twenty metrics buried in a report nobody has time to read properly. The habit matters more than the tool — a simple spreadsheet updated religiously beats an elaborate dashboard nobody opens after the first quarter.

The right set isn't identical for every business. A services firm should weight utilisation and debtor days more heavily than a retailer, who cares more about stock turn and gross margin by product line. What's constant across all of them is the underlying discipline: pick the handful of numbers that would actually change a decision if they moved, track them the same way every month, and treat a shift in the trend as the trigger to ask why — not wait for it to show up as a cash flow surprise a quarter later.
