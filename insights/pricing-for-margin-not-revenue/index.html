Pricing & margin

Pricing for margin, not just revenue: the review most SMEs skip

Averaged margin hides winners and losers. A proper pricing review usually finds more profit than a cost cut does.

Ask most SME owners how pricing was set and the honest answer is usually some version of "it's what we've always charged, plus a bit for inflation." The number wasn't wrong when it was set. It's just rarely been checked against what's actually happened to costs, mix and competitors since — and pricing is one of the few levers in a business that can lift profit without needing a single extra sale. A 5–8% price increase, held without losing meaningful volume, usually moves straight through to the bottom line in a way that a cost-cutting program of the same size never quite manages, because most of the cost base doesn't move with it.

The reason this gets missed isn't carelessness. It's that most management reporting shows one blended margin number for the whole business, and a blended number is a terrible place to look for a pricing problem — it hides exactly the detail that would reveal one.

Why the average hides the problem

A business with an overall gross margin that looks perfectly healthy can still have a meaningful share of its revenue coming from clients, products or jobs that barely cover their own cost to deliver. Averaging across the lot means the strong lines are quietly subsidising the weak ones, and nobody can see it without breaking the number apart. The fix isn't complicated, but it does take deliberately pulling margin by client, by service line, or by job — not just the company-wide figure — and ranking it. It's common for this exercise to turn up a tail of work that's been priced on an old assumption, won on a discount nobody ever revisited, or scoped years ago for a client whose requirements have since grown without the price following. None of that shows up in a single blended line; all of it shows up the moment the number gets split.

What a proper review actually covers

Cost first, because a price review built on outdated costs just re-anchors the same mistake: wages, materials, software and overheads all move over time, and if the pricing model hasn't been rebuilt to reflect current cost-to-serve, the margin on paper and the margin in reality have already drifted apart. Then the method — cost-plus pricing, where a margin is simply added onto cost, works fine for standardised, low-differentiation work, but it leaves real money on the table anywhere the business is actually delivering more value than the cost base implies. Value-based pricing, anchored to what the outcome is worth to the client rather than what it cost to produce, is harder to set and easier to defend once it's set properly. Most SMEs default to cost-plus everywhere, including the handful of relationships where value-based pricing would be the more honest — and more profitable — way to charge. And finally, the uncomfortable question: which clients or products would the business happily lose at today's price, and which ones is it quietly afraid to reprice because of who the client is, not what the numbers say?

None of this is an argument for aggressive, across-the-board increases, which can cost more in lost volume and client goodwill than they recover in margin. It's an argument for treating pricing as a number that needs the same periodic scrutiny as debtor days or stock turn — checked against current cost and current value, not left running on an assumption set years ago. The gross margin trend most businesses already track monthly is the signal that a pricing review is overdue; a proper review, done by service line rather than in aggregate, is usually what actually explains why it moved. For a business thinking about what raises its value rather than just this year's profit, a pricing model that holds up under scrutiny is one of the quieter ones — a buyer or lender can tell the difference between margin that's been deliberately built and margin that's simply whatever volume happened to produce.
