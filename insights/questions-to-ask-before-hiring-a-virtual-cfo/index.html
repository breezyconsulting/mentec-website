Virtual CFO

The questions worth asking before you hire a virtual CFO

Scope, seniority and incentives predict how an engagement goes far better than a rate card does.

Every virtual CFO conversation eventually covers the same ground — scope, cost, availability. The businesses that end up satisfied with the engagement a year in are usually the ones that asked a slightly different set of questions before hiring a virtual CFO, back when a polished proposal hadn't yet made the decision feel more settled than it actually was. None of these questions have one right answer. What matters is getting a straight one, and noticing when an answer is vague in a place it should be specific.

Questions about scope and seniority

Is this genuine CFO-level experience, built across more than one business and at least one genuinely difficult year, or a senior bookkeeper operating under a CFO title? What's actually included in the engagement — board packs and lender reporting only, or does it extend into pricing decisions, capital structure and the execution that follows a strategy, not just the strategy itself? And who does the actual work: the person running the introductory call, or a more junior team member once the retainer is signed and the relationship settles into its routine? The gap between those answers is one of the more common sources of disappointment six months into an engagement, and it rarely shows up until then.

Questions about cadence and involvement

What does availability actually mean — a fixed handful of hours booked into a calendar each month, or genuine access when something urgent lands in a week that wasn't scheduled for it? What happens in the first 90 days specifically — what gets reviewed, what gets rebuilt, and what should be visibly different by the end of it, rather than a general assurance that things will improve? And what does ongoing reporting look like — a call and a slide deck once a quarter, or numbers the business can actually act on between conversations? A fractional CFO who's spread thin across too many clients tends to reveal it here first, well before it shows up anywhere in the numbers.

The question most owners skip

How is the advisor actually paid, and what does that structure reward? Most virtual CFO arrangements are billed the same way regardless of outcome — a retainer or an hourly rate that's owed whether the business's position improves or doesn't. That's not a disqualifying answer on its own, but it's worth knowing, because it shapes what gets recommended and how long an advisor tends to stay engaged once the strategy work is finished and the harder, less interesting execution work begins. An arrangement where part of the advisor's own return depends on the business's enterprise value — an equity stake alongside a reduced retainer, for instance — answers that question differently, and it's a fair thing to ask any prospective advisor directly: how is your own compensation structured, and what does that mean for what you're likely to recommend?

None of this replaces an actual conversation about the business itself, its numbers and where it's stuck. But asking these questions first, rather than after a proposal has already shaped the conversation, tends to surface the differences that predict whether the engagement actually works — not just the ones printed on the rate card.
